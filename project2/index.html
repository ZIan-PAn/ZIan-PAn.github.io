Fun with Filters and Frequencies

I use small filters to find edges. Then I use image frequencies to sharpen photos, combine images, and blend their borders.

PART 1 / FILTERS AND EDGES

Small filters reveal changes.

Each filter uses nearby pixels to make a new value. A box filter finds the average. A derivative filter responds where brightness changes.

1.1

Build convolution from scratch

I used a grayscale portrait to compare two NumPy methods with scipy.signal.convolve2d. Both methods flip the kernel, add a border of zeros, and keep the output the same size as the input.

O(x,y)= ∑u=−rr ∑v=−ss I(x−u,y−v) K(u,v)

I center the kernel on each output pixel. I treat pixels beyond the image edge as zero.

Speed and image borders. The first method uses four loops. It performs every multiplication and addition in Python, so it takes longer as the image or filter grows. The second method uses two loops and lets NumPy do the math for each image patch. SciPy performs the same convolution in optimized code. Each method adds a border of zeros. Their results look the same, with only tiny rounding differences.
1.2

Directional differences reveal edges

I applied Dx and Dy to the cameraman image. Dx marks brightness changes from left to right. Dy marks changes from top to bottom. Together they show edge strength.

M=Ix2+Iy2 E(x,y)={1M>T0M≤T

The threshold T keeps only stronger changes in the edge map.

1.3

Smooth the image before finding edges

Raw differences also respond to small noise. I first blurred the image with a 9 × 9 Gaussian filter at σ = 2. Then I compared two methods: blur the image before taking a derivative, or use one filter that combines the blur and derivative. This second method uses a derivative of Gaussian, or DoG, filter.

G=g×gT Dx∗(G∗I)=(Dx∗G)∗I Dy∗(G∗I)=(Dy∗G)∗I

I built G from cv2.getGaussianKernel and its transpose. The two DoG filters are Dx ∗ G and Dy ∗ G.

What changed? Compared with the raw differences in 1.2, smoothing removes small background marks and keeps the cameraman outline and camera clear. Blurring before the derivative and using the combined DoG filter give the same edge map here. Both paths use the same output mode and zero padding.

PART 2.1 / SHARPENING

Bring fine detail back.

A Gaussian blur keeps broad shapes and softens fine detail. I subtract the blur from the original to get a detail layer. Then I add some of that layer back to make edges clearer.

Uα=(1+α)δ−αG Isharp=I∗Uα=I+α(I−I∗G)

δ is the impulse filter, G is a Gaussian, and α controls how much detail I add.

Limits of sharpening

Sharpening makes edges stronger, but it cannot bring back detail lost to blur. A larger α also makes noise and halos easier to see.

I use a 7 × 7 Gaussian with σ = 1 for the Taj image. The second photo uses a 31 × 31 Gaussian with σ = 5.

PART 2.2 / HYBRID IMAGES

One image can show two views.

A hybrid combines fine detail from one aligned image with broad shapes from another. The detail stands out up close. The broad shapes stand out when the image is small or far away.

H=(Ihigh−Gσhigh∗Ihigh)+Gσlow∗Ilow

The first term keeps fine detail. The second term keeps broad shapes.

PARTS 2.3 AND 2.4 / STACKS AND BLENDING

Blend each scale with a soft mask.

A Gaussian stack keeps the image size as each level becomes smoother. A Laplacian stack stores the detail removed between levels. I blur the mask at each scale, blend the matching details, and add them together.

2.3

Gaussian and Laplacian stacks

I built seven levels for the apple and orange. I kept the full image size at every level. The last Gaussian level shows broad shapes. Each earlier Laplacian level shows detail removed by the next blur.

G0=I,Gk+1=Kσ∗Gk Lk=Gk−Gk+1,Ln−1=Gn−1

Each level has the original size. The last Laplacian level is the final blurred image.

WHAT I LEARNED

One image contains detail at many scales.

I learned to see an image as broad shapes mixed with fine detail. Blur keeps broad changes and softens small ones. Derivatives and Laplacian bands show changes at different scales. Once I separate these parts, I can sharpen a photo, combine two images, or soften a seam by blending each scale.

These tools have limits. Sharpening makes edges stronger, but it cannot bring back lost detail. A hybrid works best when the photos line up and the blur settings suit the images. A blend can hide a seam, but a wide mask may mix objects that do not fit together.
