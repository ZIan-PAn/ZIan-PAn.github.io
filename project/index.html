01 / START

Making one color image

Each glass plate shows the same scene three times. One view uses a blue filter, one green, and one red. These are gray images, not a normal color photo. The scan puts them one above another in B–G–R order. I split the scan, move the views, and join them.

How I align the images. I use a simple x–y shift. Blue is the reference, so it stays still. For every possible shift, I move the green or red view left, right, up, or down. I compare the moved view with blue and keep the best shift.

After I find the two shifts, I use them on the original gray views. I put the red view in the R channel, the green view in G, and the blue view in B. The three channels then make one color image.

This model assumes that the plate only moved in the x and y directions. It does not correct rotation, scale, or a bend in the plate. It is simple and fast when the plate stays almost still.

Main score

Normalized cross-correlation (NCC)

NCC compares two images after removing their average brightness. For each image, μ is the average pixel value. I subtract μ from every pixel, so each image is centered around zero.

The top of the formula adds the matching changes in the two images. The bottom scales the result by the size of the changes in each image. This makes the score less affected by a simple brightness change.

For every candidate shift, I calculate one NCC score. A larger score means that the two images have more similar patterns. I keep the shift with the highest score. This works well when the three color views have different brightness.

NCC(A, B) = Σ (A − μA)(B − μB) √[Σ (A − μA)2] · √[Σ (B − μB)2]

02 / PYRAMID

How the image pyramid saves time

Large scans have millions of pixels. A wide search at full size is slow because every possible shift needs a new comparison. I make both views smaller and start the search at the smallest level.

Start small. I make both views smaller by one half with area resizing. I keep resizing until the image height is below 300 pixels. This removes some small detail, but the large shapes and edges stay useful. A wide shift search is much faster on a small image.

At this smallest level, I search every integer x and y shift from −30 to +30. This is a 61 × 61 search window. The best pair of values becomes the first estimate.

At the next larger level, the image is twice as large, so I multiply both parts of the estimate by 2. I then search from 5 pixels below that predicted value to 5 pixels above it in both x and y. This is an 11 × 11 local search around the prediction.

I repeat the double-and-refine step at each larger level. The search becomes more exact as the image becomes larger, but it does not need to test the full −30 to +30 window again. When the original size is reached, the last shift is used to align the full image. For a small JPEG image below 300 pixels in height, I skip the extra levels and use the wide search directly.

Why this works

I do not use the outside border when I score a shift. In the search, I leave out a border around the image and compare the center area. This keeps empty or wrapped border pixels from changing the result. I use the same settings for all main images.

03 / BETTER FEATURES

Why I use edges

The Emir image shows a problem with raw pixels. The blue robe can be bright in one view and dark in another, even though its edges stay in the same place. A pixel score can then prefer a wrong shift.

Problem. Raw NCC works best when the same object has similar brightness in all three views. This is not true for strong colors, such as Emir’s blue robe. A blue robe may be bright in the blue view but much darker in the red view.

My change. I use the Sobel gradient. Sobel looks at a small group of nearby pixels. Its x part measures a left-to-right change, and its y part measures an up-and-down change. Large values show an edge or a strong texture change.

I combine the two parts into one gradient size for every pixel. I make one gradient image for B, G, and R. I then use the same pyramid search on these three gradient images. The search uses edge strength to find the shifts, instead of using raw brightness alone.

G = √(Gx2 + Gy2)

Why it helps. A bright-to-dark change can still have a strong edge. The robe, turban, doors, and floor can line up even when their brightness is different. After finding the shifts, I apply those shifts to the original gray views to make the final color image. The gradient image is only used for matching.

What failed

Emir is the clearest failure of the raw-pixel search. The problem is the brightness, not only the shift model. The gradient search changes the input used to measure a match, so it fixes much of this problem. It does not remove all scan marks, and it cannot fix movement that is not a simple x–y shift.

01

Better features, used in alignment

Why it is useful. Raw brightness is not always a good way to compare the three views. A colored object can change brightness after a different filter is used, even when the object did not move.

I use the Sobel gradient to make a new image that shows edges and texture changes. I make this image for each of the three views. I then find the shifts with NCC and the image pyramid.

After the shifts are found, I do not use the gradient image as the final color image. I move the original gray views with the new shifts and use them as R, G, and B. This keeps the color image while using a better signal for alignment.

02

Automatic cropping

Problem. After alignment, the plate can still have black, white, or colored borders. A fixed crop is not good because each image has a different border. A wide border can also make the color image look less clean.

Method. I use a Sobel filter on each color channel. It finds places where the image changes fast. For each pixel, I keep the strongest x and y response from the three channels. This gives one edge map that can show a border even if the border is strong in only one color.

I first set an edge threshold from the image. Then I check every column and row near the four outer sides. For a column, I count how many pixels are stronger than the threshold. This fraction is the column support. I do the same for rows. A real border should have strong support across much of a row or column, not just one small mark.

I search only in the outer 12% of the image. I accept a possible border when its support is above 60%. For the left and top, I choose the supported line farthest inside. For the right and bottom, I choose the supported line nearest the inside. I then add a small 1% safety margin to remove the colored edge too.

support(x) = strong edge pixels in column x / all pixels in column x
color channelsSobel gradientsrow / column supportinside crop

Limit. Some marks on the plate also look like edges, but they do not continue across a full row or column. The support test reduces this problem, but it is not perfect. The crop removes the large side borders, while a thin top or bottom edge can remain.

03

Better color mapping

Problem. I first put the three old views into modern R, G, and B. This is a useful start, but one view may use only part of the display range. The result can look flat if most pixels are packed into a small range.

Method. For each channel, I sort its pixel values. P1 is the value near the bottom 1% of the pixels, and P99 is the value near the top 1%. I use these two values as the useful range.

I map P1 to 0 and P99 to 1. Values below P1 become 0, and values above P99 become 1. This is called clipping. A few very dark or very bright scan pixels then have less effect on the whole image.

I′ = clip(I − P1(I)P99(I) − P1(I), 0, 1)

Limit. This changes the display values, but it does not find the true old filter colors. It can also make a channel look too strong if its useful range is very small. A color chart would be needed for real color correction.

04

Automatic white balance

Problem. The image can look too red, green, or blue even when it is aligned. The three old filters do not have the same average response, so direct channel assignment can leave a global color cast.

Method. I find the average value of R, G, and B. I use their average as gray. Then I scale each channel toward that gray value. A channel with a low mean is made brighter, and a channel with a high mean is made darker.

The scale for a channel is its target gray mean divided by its current mean. I apply this one scale to every pixel in that channel. I then clip values outside 0–1, so the result stays in the valid image range.

channel scale = gray mean / channel mean

Limit. Gray world is only a guess. It assumes that the whole scene is close to neutral on average. A real green forest or a blue sky can be changed even when its color is real.

05

Automatic contrasting with CLAHE

Problem. One global contrast change may not show both dark and bright parts well. If I increase contrast for a dark area, a bright sky may lose detail. If I protect the sky, the dark area may stay flat.

Method. I first change the image from RGB to HSV. HSV separates color from brightness. I use CLAHE only on the V, or value, channel, so the color and saturation are not changed directly.

CLAHE divides the V channel into an 8 × 8 grid of small tiles. It makes a histogram for each tile and uses the local cumulative histogram to spread the values. It also clips very tall histogram bins, so one small group of pixels cannot create an extreme contrast change. Neighboring tiles are joined smoothly, which reduces hard blocks.

After this local contrast step, I put the changed V channel back with the original H and S channels. I then change the image from HSV back to RGB.

new value = (L − 1) × CDF(old value)
RGB → HSVchange V onlyHSV → RGB

Limit. It can also make dust and small scan marks easier to see. It helps visibility in local areas, but it is not full photo restoration and it cannot repair missing image detail.
